WHY SHELTER MATTERS

Shelter Is Survival.

In the mountains, a good shelter is the difference between a cold night and a dangerous one. Wind steals body heat far faster than still air, and a single night out in a storm without protection can cause hypothermia or frostbite.

Mountaineers use different shelters for different heights and seasons: tents on approach treks and high camps, stone huts on popular routes, light bivouac bags for emergencies and snow caves when nothing else is available.

Below you can learn how each type works, how to choose a safe campsite, and browse the curated base camps and lodges we offer across the Himalayas.

TYPES OF MOUNTAIN SHELTER

Four Ways To Spend The Night.

Each shelter has a place. Experienced climbers know when to use every one.

Four-Season Tents

Strong poles, low profiles and snow flaps let these tents survive high winds and snow loads at base camp and high camps.

Mountain Huts

Stone or wooden huts on popular routes offer bunks, meals and a warm room. Book early in busy seasons.

Bivouac

A "bivvy" is a light waterproof bag or a simple tarp. Climbers use it on long routes or in emergencies when they cannot reach camp.

Snow Caves & Igloos

Snow is an excellent insulator. A well-built snow cave can stay near 0°C inside while the air outside is far below freezing.

CHOOSING A CAMPSITE

Where To Pitch.

  • Stay out of the line of fire. Avoid slopes below cornices, steep snow and loose rock, and stay away from avalanche paths.
  • Find shelter from wind. Use boulders, moraine ridges or a snow wall to block the prevailing wind.
  • Look for flat, durable ground. Stamp down snow or clear stones, and avoid hollows where cold air and water collect.
  • Stay near water, but not too near. Camp at least 60 metres from lakes and streams to protect them and avoid flooding.
  • Anchor everything. Use snow stakes, rocks or buried "deadman" anchors. A loose tent can blow away in seconds.

STAYING WARM OVERNIGHT

Five Habits For A Warm Night.

Small habits make a big difference when temperatures fall after dark.

01

Insulate From The Ground

The ground steals more heat than the air. Use a closed-cell foam mat under an inflatable mat.

02

Change Into Dry Layers

Sweaty clothes freeze. Keep a dry base layer and socks only for sleeping.

03

Eat And Drink Before Bed

A warm drink and a high-calorie snack help your body produce heat through the night.

04

Ventilate The Tent

Open a vent to reduce condensation, and never run a stove inside a closed tent because of carbon monoxide.

05

Keep Batteries Warm

Put your phone, headlamp batteries and water bottle inside your sleeping bag.
