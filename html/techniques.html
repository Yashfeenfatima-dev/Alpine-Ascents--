Rope work, snow and ice skills, glacier travel and acclimatization: the techniques that bring climbers home safely.

SKILL BEFORE SUMMIT

Technique Is What Keeps Climbers Alive.

Strength and fitness help, but technique is what lets a climber move efficiently, protect a fall and make good decisions when conditions change. Most mountaineering accidents involve a basic skill that was forgotten, rushed or never learned.

The techniques on this page are grouped by terrain: rock and rope work, snow and ice, glacier travel, high altitude and navigation. Each one should be learned with a qualified instructor and practised on easy ground before it is used on a real mountain.

Remember: reading about a technique is a starting point, not training. Practise every skill until you can do it while cold, tired and wearing gloves.

PRACTICE MAKES SAFE

CORE TECHNIQUES

Twelve Skills Every Mountaineer Learns.

Choose a terrain to filter the list.

Rock & Rope

Footwork & Balance

Good climbers climb with their legs, not their arms. Keep your weight over your feet and move one limb at a time.

  • Keep three points of contact on hard ground
  • Use the edge of the shoe on small holds
  • Keep arms straight to save strength
Rock & Rope

Knots

A handful of knots, tied perfectly every time, cover almost every situation on the mountain.

  • Figure-eight follow-through to tie in
  • Clove hitch for anchors
  • Prusik for ascending and backups
Rock & Rope

Belaying

The belayer manages the rope so a falling climber is held. A belay device creates friction and locks the rope when it is pulled.

  • Never let go of the brake hand
  • Check knot, harness and device before every climb
  • Use clear calls such as "On belay?" and "Climb on"
Rock & Rope

Rappelling (Abseiling)

Climbers slide down a fixed rope with a friction device. It is a common way down steep ground and one of the riskiest moments of a climb.

  • Tie stopper knots in both rope ends
  • Use a friction-hitch backup below the device
  • Check the anchor before you commit your weight
Snow & Ice

Crampon Technique

Crampons are metal spikes fixed to boots. On gentle slopes climbers place all points flat (French technique). On steep ice they kick in the front points (German or front-pointing technique).

  • Walk with feet slightly apart to avoid snagging trousers
  • Keep the whole sole flat on moderate slopes
Snow & Ice

Ice Axe Self-Arrest

If you slip on a snow slope, you have only seconds to stop. Self-arrest means rolling onto the axe and driving its pick into the snow with your body weight.

  • Hold the axe in the uphill hand
  • Keep crampon points up while arresting
  • Practise on a safe slope with a clear run-out
Snow & Ice

Ice Climbing

Steep ice is climbed with two ice tools and front points. Protection comes from ice screws that the leader places and the second removes.

  • Swing from the elbow, not the shoulder
  • Read ice colour and texture for quality
Glacier

Roped Glacier Travel

Glaciers hide crevasses under snow bridges. Teams of two to four climbers tie into one rope with spacing between them, so a fall into a crevasse can be held.

  • Keep the rope fairly tight between climbers
  • Cross snow bridges early in the day when they are frozen
  • Probe suspicious snow with an axe or pole
Glacier

Crevasse Rescue

If a partner falls, the team builds an anchor in the snow and uses a pulley system, often a "Z-pulley", to haul them out.

  • Carry prusiks, slings and pulleys on every glacier
  • Practise the system before the trip, not during it
High Altitude

Acclimatization

The body needs days to adapt to thin air. The golden rule is "climb high, sleep low": go higher during the day and return lower to sleep.

  • Above 3,000 m, raise your sleeping height by only 300–500 m a day
  • Add a rest day every 3–4 days
  • Descend if symptoms get worse
High Altitude

Fixed-Line Ascending

On big expedition routes, ropes are fixed to the mountain. Climbers clip in and slide a mechanical ascender (often called a jumar) up the line.

  • Always stay clipped in with a safety lanyard
  • Clip past each anchor before unclipping
  • Never trust an old rope without checking it
Navigation

Map, Compass & GPS

Cloud and snow can hide every landmark within minutes. Climbers must be able to find their position and follow a bearing without relying on a phone.

  • Read contour lines to understand slope and terrain
  • Set a bearing before visibility drops
  • Carry spare batteries for GPS devices

CLEAR COMMUNICATION

Climbing Calls.

Wind, distance and rock walls make it hard to hear. Climbing partners use short, standard calls so there is never any doubt about what is happening on the rope.

Agree on the calls before you start, and always answer a call so your partner knows it was heard.

HOW TO LEARN

A Safe Learning Path.

Most climbers follow a path like this over several seasons. Do not skip steps.

  1. 1

    Hiking & Scrambling

    Build fitness, learn to read the weather and move confidently on rough ground.

  2. 2

    Indoor & Rock Climbing

    Learn knots, belaying and movement on rock with an instructor or a club.

  3. 3

    Snow Skills Course

    Practise crampons, ice axe self-arrest and avalanche awareness on a supervised slope.

  4. 4

    Glacier & Alpine Routes

    Climb easy glaciated peaks in roped teams and practise crevasse rescue.

  5. 5

    High-Altitude Expedition

    Only after years of experience: acclimatization, expedition logistics and leadership.
